Great question to poke at — and yes, this territory has been explored, some of
it quite deeply. Let me give you the map, because it splits into a shallow
layer (which explains your base-2/base-6 observations) and a deep layer (which
is where the genuinely open, hard stuff lives).

## The shallow layer: last digits are just congruences

The last digit of n in base B is n mod B. So "multiples of 2 are easy to see
in base 2" is the statement "n mod 2 = 0", and every pattern you can see in
the trailing digits of a base-B representation is a congruence condition mod a
power of B. Your twin-prime instinct actually shows up beautifully here: every
twin prime pair above (3,5) has the form (6k−1, 6k+1), so in base 6, all
primes > 3 end in 1 or 5, and twin primes are exactly the pairs ending in 5
and 1 that straddle a multiple of 10₆. That feels like a discovery the first
time you see it, but it's equivalent to wheel factorization / Dirichlet's
arithmetic-progressions setup — and analytic number theory already speaks that
language fluently. The Hardy–Littlewood conjecture, sieve theory (Zhang,
Maynard, Polymath8 on bounded prime gaps) all work with congruence structure
directly, so single-base last-digit patterns don't add new information beyond
what sieves already use.

## The deep layer: this is a real field, with real theorems

Where it gets interesting is *whole* digit strings and *multiple* bases at
once:

- Automatic sequences and Cobham's theorem. A set of integers is "B-automatic"
  if a finite automaton reading base-B digits can recognize it. Cobham's
  theorem (1969) is exactly your multi-base idea: a set automatic in two
  multiplicatively independent bases (like 2 and 3, but *not* 2 and 4) must be
  eventually periodic — i.e., boring. And Minsky–Papert (1966) proved the
  primes are not automatic in any base. So there's a hard no-go theorem: no
  finite-state description of the primes' digits exists, in any base or
  combination of bases. This is both the bad news and the proof that your
  question is a real one people took seriously.
- Büchi arithmetic + Walnut. Statements about numbers expressible via their
  base-k digits (in a certain logical language) are *decidable* — a program
  can literally prove or refute them automatically. Jeffrey Shallit's group
  uses a tool called Walnut (Java, scriptable) to machine-prove theorems about
  base-k representations. This is the closest existing thing to "devising
  proofs using properties between bases" — with the caveat above that
  primality itself lives outside its expressive power.
- Digits of primes is an active frontier. Mauduit–Rivat (2010) proved the sum
  of binary digits of primes is equidistributed (a ~40-year-old Gelfond
  problem). Bourgain got primes with a positive proportion of prescribed
  binary digits. James Maynard — the same Maynard who holds the record on
  small prime gaps — proved there are infinitely many primes with no digit 7
  in base 10 (2016). These proofs are *hard*, Fields-Medal-level hard, which
  tells you the difficulty calibration of digit-based approaches.
- Multi-base rigidity. How the *same* number looks in base 2 vs base 3 is the
  subject of Furstenberg's ×2×3 conjecture and normality questions (e.g.,
  nobody can prove √2 is normal in any base). The theme, over and over:
  independent bases are "orthogonal" in a way that makes joint statements
  either rigid (Cobham) or wide open (Furstenberg).

The honest summary: your intuition that multi-base structure is underexplored
is half right — it's a smaller field than sieve theory, and the joint-bases
world has huge open problems. But the specific hope that primes get
"predictable" across bases runs into Minsky–Papert/Cobham: the predictable
part collapses to congruences, and everything beyond that is provably
non-finite-state.

## Python tooling for playing with this

- sympy — sympy.ntheory.digits.digits(n, b) for arbitrary-base digit lists,
  plus isprime, primerange, nextprime. Best all-rounder for experimenting.
- gmpy2 — mpz(n).digits(base) up to base 62, very fast for big numbers.
- primesieve (pip) — generates primes orders of magnitude faster than sympy.
- numpy.base_repr(n, base) — quick and dirty, bases ≤ 36.
- automata-lib (pip) — build/manipulate finite automata in Python.
- Walnut (Java, free) — the actual "prove theorems about base-k
  representations" tool.
- SageMath — if you outgrow pip packages.

A fun first experiment: generate primes up to 10^7 with primesieve, and for
pairs of bases (2,3), (2,4), (6,10), look at the joint distribution of leading
digits, digit sums, and digit-string statistics of p and p+2. You'll
rediscover Mauduit–Rivat-style equidistribution empirically — and seeing *how*
uniformly random the digits look is itself the best intuition for why the
conjecture is hard. Happy to scaffold that playground script if you want it.

## The "i ≥ 2" claim: partly false, partly a two-line proof

Define g(B) = (next prime after B) − B. First: g is always well-defined and
finite — that's Euclid (there's always a next prime), and Bertrand's postulate
even gives g(B) < B. But "always ≥ 2" depends on what B ranges over:

- B any integer: false. g(4) = 1 (next prime is 5), g(2) = 1, g(10) = 1. In
  fact g(B) = 1 exactly when B+1 is prime.
- B an odd prime: true, and here's the whole proof: p and the next prime q are
  both odd (since q > p ≥ 3, q ≠ 2), so q − p is a difference of two odd
  numbers, hence even and positive, hence ≥ 2. ∎

So the floor of your "imaginary part" is elementary. Everything interesting
about g lives in the other direction — and that's where you should notice
something exciting: the twin prime conjecture is precisely the statement that
g(p) = 2 happens infinitely often. Your construction didn't wander near the
conjecture; it re-derived its exact statement. The function g(p_n) = p_{n+1} −
p_n is the prime gap function, one of the most intensely studied objects in
mathematics: Zhang/Maynard/Polymath proved g(p) ≤ 246 infinitely often, Cramér
conjectured g(p) = O((log p)²), and the twin prime conjecture is "liminf g = 2."

## Why "B + i·g(B)" doesn't form a plane — and what that teaches

The complex plane isn't powerful because it has two coordinates. It's powerful
because i² = −1 makes the pairs a *field* whose arithmetic is compatible in
both coordinates, which unlocks calculus (holomorphic functions, contour
integration). For your pairs to be a number system, you'd need addition and
multiplication laws where the second coordinate behaves — but g wrecks that:
g(a + b) has no relationship to g(a) and g(b). g(8) = 3, g(9) = 2, g(17) = 2;
no law connects them. So {(B, g(B))} is the *graph of a function*, not a plane
— a new coordinate system for facts you already had, with no new symmetry to
exploit. This is the general test for whether a proposed structure can prove
anything: does it come with operations or symmetries that constrain the
object? Renaming g as an imaginary part adds a notation, not a constraint.

But here's the encouraging part: your instinct — "embed the primes in a richer
2D structure where analysis can see the gaps" — is exactly the strategy that
has produced every deep result on prime distribution. It just uses a different
embedding:

- The Riemann zeta function lives on the honest complex plane, and its zeros
  encode prime distribution completely.
- Montgomery's pair correlation (1973) is the spooky one for you: the
  statistics of gaps between *zeta zeros* match the eigenvalue gaps of random
  Hermitian matrices (GUE) — a genuinely new structure, discovered over tea
  with Freeman Dyson, that imported an entire field (random matrix theory)
  into prime gaps. That's what a productive "new plane" looks like: it arrived
  with its own theorems.
- Gaussian integers Z[i] are the literal "complex plane of integers," with
  their own primes — that structure is a field-with-symmetry, which is why it
  proves things (e.g., which primes are sums of two squares).

## A concrete way to play with your object anyway

Plot g(n) for n up to 10^8: you'll see the gap record-holders grow like log²,

Lemke Oliver–Soundararajan "primes hate to repeat their last digit"
phenomenon, 2016 — discovered exactly this way, by looking at
consecutive-prime statistics someone assumed were boring. That discovery is
the proof-of-concept that staring at g with fresh eyes can still find new
mathematics — it found some in 2016.

This is the best turn yet, because you've now wandered into a field that
genuinely exists, is gorgeous, and is much less famous than it deserves to be:
exotic numeration systems. Non-integer bases, negative bases, and complex
bases all make rigorous sense, and your closing intuition — that two "broken"
bases can combine back into a working one — has at least three precise
instantiations in the literature.

## What a base actually is

Positional notation is just the claim that every number n can be written
n = Σ dₖ·βᵏ where the digits dₖ come from some finite set D. Nothing in that
formula requires β to be a positive integer — it requires the *pair* (β, D)
to have existence and (some) uniqueness of expansions. Change the pair:

- Base −2, digits {0,1} (negabinary): every integer, positive or negative,
  gets a unique representation with no sign symbol. −3 is 1101₋₂. Old enough
  that it appeared in a 1957 computer (Poland's UMC-1).
- Base φ (golden ratio), digits {0,1}: the founding example of β-expansions,
  introduced by Rényi (1957) and Parry (1960). Legal strings never contain
  "11" (because φ² = φ + 1 — the carry rule *is* the minimal polynomial).
  Every integer has a finite expansion: 2 = 10.01_φ.
- Base 3/2, digits {0,1,2}: perfectly functional, and arithmetic in it is
  mysteriously hard — its difficulty is cousin to the Collatz problem and
  Mahler's Z-number problem.
- Base i−1, digits {0,1} (Penney, 1965): every Gaussian integer gets a unique
  representation. No sign, no separate imaginary part — one bit string per
  point of Z[i].
- Base 2i, digits {0,1,2,3} (Knuth's "quater-imaginary," 1960): every complex
  number, real and imaginary parts together in a single digit string.

So "it doesn't really make sense to represent numbers that way" — it does, and
Knuth got there in 1960 as an undergrad. The general theory is called
canonical number systems (Kátai–Szabó, 1975).

## Your "two broken bases combine into a working base" idea — three real versions

1. Knuth's interleaving. Take a quater-imaginary (base 2i) digit string and
   split it into even-position and odd-position digits. The even-position
   digits are exactly a base −4 representation of the real part, and the
   odd-position digits are a base −4 representation of (imaginary part ÷ 2).
   One imaginary-base number *is* two negative-integer-base numbers braided
   together. That's your idea, verbatim, sixty years old.

2. Conjugate collapse. The base β = i−1 has complex conjugate β̄ = −i−1, and
   β·β̄ = 2. Norms turn complex-base structure back into honest base-2
   structure.

3. Pisot conjugates and Rauzy's construction — the deepest one. Why does
   arithmetic in base φ behave? Because φ's algebraic conjugate is −1/φ,
   inside the unit circle. The modern viewpoint (Rauzy, Thurston) embeds each
   number by all its conjugate coordinates at once — and in that
   higher-dimensional space the legal expansions form a lattice tiling whose
   tiles are fractals (Rauzy fractals). The "imaginary part" you were reaching
   for exists, and it's the Galois-conjugate coordinate: the invisible axis
   you have to add before a non-integer base becomes geometrically periodic
   and computable. Frougny proved that in any Pisot base, addition and
   digit-normalization are computable by finite automata — which loops us all
   the way back to the automata/Walnut world from your first question.

One more piece of geometry worth having in your head: for complex bases, the
set of numbers with expansion 0.d₁d₂d₃… is a fractal — for base i−1 it's the
twindragon, a space-filling tile that tessellates the plane. The fractional
parts of exotic bases are where dragon curves come from.

## Honest note on the prime connection

This field connects primes only weakly. Where it's rich is fractal geometry,
ergodic theory (the β-shift and Parry measure), and automata. So as a
twin-prime attack vector it's a long shot — but as "a plethora of math
involving representations in unusual bases," this is exactly the plethora, and
parts of it (rational bases like 3/2, which digit sets admit which β) are wide
open at an amateur-accessible level.

There's no polished Python library for this — the honest answer is you write
the greedy Rényi algorithm and complex-base digit extraction yourself in ~40
lines, which is a feature: the algorithms *are* the understanding. I'd suggest
a toolkit that (1) expands any number in base β for arbitrary real/complex β,
(2) verifies Knuth's interleaving numerically, and (3) plots the twindragon.

## The fold is real: cut-and-project

The rigorous version of "predictable pre-fold structure that looks complicated
post-fold" is a cut-and-project scheme: take a perfectly periodic lattice in a
higher-dimensional space, slice it with a strip, and project ("fold") the
strip down to the line. The result is a model set — aperiodic, never
repeating, yet completely deterministic, with sharp diffraction peaks like a
crystal. The Fibonacci quasicrystal is the fold of Z² through a strip of slope
1/φ, Penrose tilings are the fold of a lattice in five dimensions, and —
closing the loop with last time — the Rauzy fractal is exactly the window of
such a scheme: the fractal you fold *through*. The reference tome is Baake &
Grimm, "Aperiodic Order."

So the sharp question is yours, precisely posed: are the primes a fold of
something periodic?

## The answer: almost — and the "almost" is the whole ballgame

Strictly, no: model sets are Delone (gaps bounded), and prime gaps are
provably unbounded (n!+2, n!+3, … are all composite). The primes can't
literally be a cut-and-project set.

But in 2018, Torquato, Zhang, and de Courcy-Ireland computed the primes'
diffraction pattern — treating primes as atoms and doing the scattering
calculation physicists do to crystals — and found that in dilated intervals
the primes are effectively limit-periodic: their structure factor shows an
infinite, self-similar hierarchy of sharp Bragg-like peaks. Limit-periodic
means "an infinite superposition of periodic structures at ever-larger
periods" — which is a *different kind of fold*: an inverse limit rather than a
projection. The primes diffract like a new species of quasicrystal. Your
"we'd see the primes as predictable and repeatable within that structure" is,
at the level of diffraction, a published empirical fact.

And we know exactly where those peaks come from: the wheel hierarchy. Primes
avoid residue 0 mod 2, mod 3, mod 5, … — each wheel is a periodic structure,
and the primes are what survives every wheel simultaneously. The natural
"pre-fold" space for an infinite tower of compatible congruences is the
profinite integers Ẑ (equivalently: numbers written in *primorial base*, where
"coprime to the first k primes" is a condition on the first k digits — a
numeration system again). And here's a genuinely beautiful known theorem: the
closure of the primes in Ẑ is exactly the set of units — and that statement is
*equivalent to Dirichlet's theorem* on primes in arithmetic progressions. In
the pre-fold space, the primes fill out a perfectly clean, completely
describable set. Maximum predictability, exactly as you hoped.

## Why this doesn't finish the job — the parity barrier

Now the honest turn. The fold you'd invert captures precisely the *congruence*
information about primes — and analytic number theorists have a name for the
ceiling on what congruence information can prove: Selberg's parity problem.
Sieve methods, which are exactly the machinery of "intersect infinitely many
wheels," provably cannot distinguish numbers with an odd number of prime
factors from numbers with an even number. Any argument using only
wheel/congruence/pre-fold structure hits this wall — it can prove there are
infinitely many n such that n(n+2) has at most 4 prime factors (Chen's
theorem, 1973, the closest anyone has come to twin primes), but it cannot, by
itself, get to "both prime." Twin primes lives in the *fiber* of the fold: the
part of the primes' structure that the profinite shadow forgets. Every modern
breakthrough (Zhang, Maynard) works by injecting non-congruence input —
equidistribution estimates whose ancestry traces to zeta and L-function zeros
— into the sieve.

So the refined status of your idea: the pre-fold predictable structure exists
(units of Ẑ / limit-periodic diffraction), the fold is the sieve, its
predictions are hard theorems (Dirichlet, Chen), and the twin prime conjecture
is the statement that the primes are equidistributed in the fold's fibers just
slightly more uniformly than anyone can prove. The Hardy–Littlewood constant
for twin primes — Π(1 − 1/(p−1)²) — is literally computed from the pre-fold
structure; it predicts the *density* of twins flawlessly in numerical tests.
What's missing is not the structure. It's a proof that the fibers don't
conspire.

The experiment that would make all of this tactile: numerically compute the
structure factor of the primes up to 10^8 and watch the self-similar Bragg
peak hierarchy emerge, side by side with the same computation for a true model
set (Fibonacci quasicrystal) and for random points — three diffraction
patterns, and the primes visibly sitting between order and noise. I can build
that if you want it.

Let me play this one straight, because there's a seductive trap in it — and

## What the construction would literally be

Space-filling curves *are* base machines: the Hilbert/Sierpinski curve maps n
to a plane point by reading n's base-4 digits and folding at each scale — it's
the same "digits drive geometry" mechanism as base i−1 and the twindragon. So
your construction is well-defined with off-the-shelf parts: send each integer
through the curve, color the primes, and twin primes become pairs of prime
cells two steps apart along the curve. Space-filling curves have a locality
theorem — points close in N land close in the plane at every scale — so twins
are geometric near-neighbors at all zoom levels, automatically. The Ulam
spiral (1963) is the folk ancestor of exactly this: primes on a square spiral,
where the famous diagonal streaks turn out to be quadratic polynomials rich in
primes — i.e., the visible structure decodes back to Hardy–Littlewood
conjectures, not past them.

## The trap: bijections don't create theorems

Here's the thing to hold onto. The map from N into the fractal is a bijective
re-encoding, and a re-encoding preserves *exactly* the information present —
so "there is always a neighboring prime pair, no matter the order/scale" is
the twin prime conjecture with new nouns. Proving it in the fractal is proving
it, full stop; the geometry gives you no purchase unless the embedding's
symmetries act on primality. And this is where the no-go theorems we've
already met bite with precision: the self-similarity of a Sierpinski-type
construction is finite-state — zooming is a digit shift, and
digit-shift-invariant sets are exactly the automatic sets. Minsky–Papert:
primes are not automatic in any base. So there is an actual theorem saying the
primes can never be an exactly self-similar subset of any such fractal
construction — the streaks you'd see are the limit-periodic sieve shadow (the
Torquato structure from last time), and the twin prime content lives entirely
in the deviation from self-similarity, which is precisely the part the
fractal's own operations can't see. Frougny's automata do addition in these
systems; they provably can't do primality.

## A subtler danger: the fractal phrasing tempts you into *false* statements

"Always a neighboring pair no matter what order" — read naturally as "every
level-k tile contains a twin pair" — is *stronger* than the twin prime
conjecture, and stronger in a way that's flat-out false: each tile at depth k
covers a specific interval of integers, and there are intervals of any length
containing no primes at all (n!+2 through n!+n), never mind twins. TPC only
claims twins occur at arbitrarily large positions, not uniformly in every
window. Geometric uniformity language ("every region, every scale") quietly
upgrades the conjecture into something refutable. Any workable fractal
statement has to be of the form "infinitely many tiles contain a pair," which
folds right back to the original.

## The checklist test, and where this instinct could still earn its keep

From the "B + i" round, the test for a new structure: does it come with
operations or symmetries that *constrain* the primes, rather than just
re-address them? Gaussian primes pass (rotation by i, norm map — and it proves
theorems: sums of two squares). Zeta's critical strip passes (the functional
equation s ↔ 1−s is a symmetry that squeezes real theorems out). Sierpinski
embeddings fail, provably, by the automaticity no-go.

But as an *instrument* rather than a proof machine, your construction is
legitimately good, and history backs that: Ulam found his spiral doodling in a
boring meeting, and Lemke Oliver–Soundararajan found the consecutive-prime
bias in 2016 by computing something everyone assumed was featureless.
Rendering primes on a Hilbert curve versus the twindragon versus the spiral,
and looking at pair-correlation anisotropy — whether prime neighbors prefer
certain directions the sieve doesn't obviously explain — is exactly the kind
of look-with-fresh-eyes computation that occasionally surfaces a real
phenomenon nobody had named. If we build anything from this thread, I'd build
that: three embeddings, primes colored, twins highlighted, plus a
pair-correlation rose plot per embedding. Say the word and I'll write it.

pattern repeats forever") into the shape mathematics can actually act on. Let
me walk the whole pipeline: detection → decoding → formalization → the proof
machinery that exists → the wall at the end, and the one known way walls like
it have broken.

## Step 1 — Detection means beating a null model, not seeing a pattern

A spike in R at some β means nothing by itself; carries and wheels guarantee
structure. "Detected" means: the twin alignment at β persists as N grows and
exceeds what prime-free structure predicts. Concretely you'd run three point
sets through the identical pipeline: (1) actual primes, (2) a Cramér surrogate
— random integers with density 1/log n, (3) a wheel surrogate — random
integers restricted to 6k±1, which carries all the congruence structure and
nothing else. Plot R(N) for N = 2^14, 2^16, …, 2^20 for all three. Almost
every spike you find will match the wheel surrogate — that's the sieve
talking, already provable, no news. A candidate discovery is a gap between the
prime curve and the wheel curve that doesn't shrink as N grows.

## Step 2 — Decode geometry back into arithmetic

Position in the fractal is a digit condition, so any persistent alignment
statement mechanically translates into: the pair (p, p+2) prefers certain
joint digit patterns. The vector z(p+2) − z(p) is determined by how adding 2
carries through p's digits, so your geometric statement becomes a statement
about the distribution of carry-lengths and digit-blocks of p at twin
positions. That's the theorem candidate — the fractal was scaffolding.

## Step 3 — The formal statement and the machinery that exists for it

The decoded claim takes a standard shape: a correlation sum like
Σ_{n≤N} Λ(n)Λ(n+2)·f(digits of n) has a specific main term, where Λ is the
von Mangoldt function and f is your digit condition. There is a real, working
toolkit for exactly this genre: Vaughan / Heath-Brown identities decompose Λ
into bilinear sums; Mauduit–Rivat's carry-propagation lemmas bound the digital
pieces; Maynard's machinery handles digit conditions of positive codimension.
If your statement survives this mill, what you get is a Hardy–Littlewood-type
equidistribution theorem — genuinely valuable, and here is the realistic best
case: a new equidistribution input is precisely the fuel the Maynard–Tao sieve
burns. Zhang's 2013 breakthrough was exactly this shape — not a twin proof,
but a new distribution estimate fed into sieve machinery, giving 70,000,000,
refined to 246. A digit-geometric equidistribution theorem nobody had could
plausibly lower that number. That is the honest ceiling of the "detect and
formalize" pathway.

## Step 4 — The wall, stated precisely, and the one known crack in walls like it

Two theorems tell you where this pathway provably ends:

- Parity barrier (Selberg): even granted the strongest plausible
  equidistribution conjecture (generalized Elliott–Halberstam), sieve methods
  get gaps ≤ 6 — and provably cannot reach 2.
- Sarnak orthogonality for automatic sequences (Müllner, 2017): any
  digit-defined, finite-state condition — which is exactly the class of
  signals your fractal can express — is asymptotically orthogonal to the
  Möbius function. Breaking parity requires correlating with μ; your signal
  class is proven to decorrelate from it in the limit. This is also why
  "neighbors repeating infinitely pre-fold" can't be the theorem: limit
  objects wash out to the wheel-adjusted uniform measure, and TPC lives
  entirely in the part that washes out. The conjecture is a statement about a
  sequence refusing to die, which no weak limit remembers.

theorem + a fundamentally non-sieve, parity-breaking idea. Does such an idea

sparser than sieves should ever handle — because that specific sequence has a
hidden bilinear structure that lets you get at μ directly. It's the existence
proof that parity walls crack when the sequence carries exceptional algebraic
structure. The dream version of your program isn't "the pattern repeats, QED"

nothing proves it isn't there.
