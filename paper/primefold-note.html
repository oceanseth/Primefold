Twin pairs of Friedlander–Iwaniec form: a census to 107, a bi-norm decomposition, and a decoded coincidence

Seth (@oceanseth, seth@voicecert.com), with Claude (Anthropic)

July 11, 2026 (v2) · code and data: github.com/oceanseth/Primefold

Abstract. Call a prime of the form p = a2 + b4 a Friedlander–Iwaniec (FI) prime. We enumerate the FI primes below 107 (13,175 of them) and those with p+2 also prime (1,654 pairs), and record three elementary observations. First, every such pair is a bi-norm pair: p is a norm from ℚ(i) by construction, while p+2 is forced by its residue mod 8 to be a norm from ℚ(√−2) or from ℚ(√2), never from ℚ(i). Second, a search for exact linear relations between the two parameterizations finds a single family, x = a, explained by the coincidence 42+2 = 2·32: the pairs p = a2+16, p+2 = a2+2·32. The relevant quartic Pell equation y2 = 8c4+1 admits no further solutions (verified for c ≤ 106), so this is the only such family. The family's count below 107 is 68, against a Hardy–Littlewood prediction of 67.9. Third, a wrong-partner permutation control shows that the entire statistical correlation between the two parameterizations (+0.145, about 5 s.e.) is attributable to this one family; with it removed, the residual is consistent with zero. None of this is deep, and we claim no novelty: the purpose of the note is to record the observations, the quantitative Hardy–Littlewood check, and reproducible code, and to explain precisely why such structure, however suggestive, cannot by itself approach the twin prime conjecture. Version 2 adds: the census extended to 109; the complete classification of pass-through folds b4+s = m y2 for shifts s ≤ 100 (revealing a second shift-2 family, a2+1, a2+3, both families confirmed against Hardy–Littlewood at 109); and first data on gaps between FI primes themselves — minimum gap 4, first at (37, 41), with 28,262 consecutive pairs within distance 246 below 109, a bounded-gaps question that current theorems do not cover.

1. Context

Friedlander and Iwaniec [4] proved that the polynomial a2 + b4 represents infinitely many primes — a landmark, because the sequence is sparse (about x3/4/log x values up to x) and the proof must break the parity barrier of sieve theory, which it does via the algebraic structure a2 + b4 = Nℚ(i)/ℚ(a + b2i). The intersection of this theorem with the twin prime conjecture — are there infinitely many FI primes p with p+2 also prime? — trivially implies the twin prime conjecture and is therefore beyond current methods; the strongest unconditional result in that direction remains Chen's theorem [2], and the strongest gap results are those of Zhang, Maynard, and Polymath [11, 8, 9]. Everything in the present note is elementary or computational. Its point is to document what the joint structure of (p, p+2) looks like below the parity barrier: which parts are forced, which parts are coincidence, and which parts are (measurably) absent.

2. Census

Below N = 107 there are 13,175 FI primes; 1,654 of them have p+2 prime. The first are 5, 17, 41, 101, 137, 197, 281, 617, 641, 821, and the largest below 107 is 9,989,921. Their distribution mod 8 is uneven in a way that is fully explained by parity of the parameters:

Proposition 1. Let p = a2 + b4 be an odd prime. Then p ≡ 1 (mod 4); moreover p ≡ 5 (mod 8) occurs exactly when a ≡ 2 (mod 4) and b is odd, and p ≡ 1 (mod 8) in all other cases.
Proof. Since p is odd, exactly one of a, b is even. If a is odd and b even: a2 ≡ 1 (mod 8) and b4 ≡ 0 (mod 16), so p ≡ 1 (mod 8). If a is even and b odd: b4 ≡ 1 (mod 16); writing a = 2c, we get a2 ≡ 0 (mod 16) for c even and a2 ≡ 4 (mod 32) for c odd, giving p ≡ 1 and p ≡ 5 (mod 8) respectively. ∎

Of the four parameter parity classes, one produces p ≡ 5 (mod 8), predicting a ratio near 3:1. Observed: 9,810 : 3,364 = 2.92 for all FI primes, and 1,227 : 427 = 2.87 for the twins — the twin condition does not measurably disturb the split.

3. The bi-norm decomposition

Proposition 2. Let p = a2+b4 and q = p+2 both be prime, p > 3. Then: (i) q ≡ 3 (mod 4), so q is inert in ℤ[i] and is never a sum of two squares; (ii) if p ≡ 1 (mod 8) then q ≡ 3 (mod 8) and q = x2 + 2y2 with x, y > 0 unique; (iii) if p ≡ 5 (mod 8) then q ≡ 7 (mod 8) and q = x2 − 2y2, with infinitely many representations.
Proof. (i) is Proposition 1 plus the classical two-squares theorem. For (ii) and (iii): a prime q is represented by x2+2y2 iff −2 is a quadratic residue mod q, i.e. q ≡ 1, 3 (mod 8), and uniqueness follows since ℚ(√−2) has class number one; it is represented by x2−2y2 iff 2 is a residue, i.e. q ≡ ±1 (mod 8), with the unit group of ℤ[√2] generating infinitely many representations. The residues in (ii), (iii) follow from Proposition 1. ∎

So the +2 shift always ejects the prime from the Gaussian world (the split prime p lands on an inert neighbor), and always deposits it in a quadratic field determined by p mod 8. Every FI twin pair is thus a pair of norms from two different quadratic fields. This is forced, costs nothing, and — this is the point of Section 5 — carries no detectable information linking the two coordinate systems beyond what congruences force.

4. The x = a family: one diophantine coincidence

For the 1,227 pairs with p ≡ 1 (mod 8) we computed the unique representation q = x2+2y2 and searched for exact relations x = |u a + v b2|, |u|, |v| ≤ 3. Exactly one relation occurs more often than stray coincidence: x = a, in 67 of the pairs — and in every instance b = 2, y = 3.

Proposition 3. In the setting of Proposition 2(ii), x = a holds iff b4 + 2 = 2y2, which forces b = 2c with y2 = 8c4 + 1. The only solution with 1 ≤ c ≤ 106 is c = 1, i.e. (b, y) = (2, 3).
Proof. Subtracting a2 from q = p+2 gives 2y2 = b4+2. For odd b, b4+2 ≡ 3 (mod 16) is odd times no power of 2 — impossible. With b = 2c: 16c4+2 = 2y2 gives y2 = 8c4+1. Solutions lie in the Pell sequence y2 − 8d2 = 1 with d = c2; the d-values 1, 6, 35, 204, 1189, … must be perfect squares, and the computation verifies only d = 1 occurs for c ≤ 106. (Equations of this shape fall to the classical methods of Ljunggren and Cohn on quartic Pell equations [7, 3]; for the census below 107 the direct enumeration suffices regardless.) ∎

The coincidence 42+2 = 2·32 therefore generates the unique family in which the +2 shift folds one norm form onto another with the parameter a passing through unchanged:

p = a2 + 16 (norm from ℚ(i)),   p+2 = a2 + 2·32 (norm from ℚ(√−2)).

Counting directly over a, the family has 68 members below 107, beginning a = 1, 5, 11, 25, 29, 41, 65, 95, i.e. the twin pairs (17, 19), (41, 43), (137, 139), (641, 643), (857, 859), (1697, 1699), (4241, 4243), (9041, 9043). (A smallest-b deduplication sees 67 of these: the member 17 = 12+24 = 42+14 is recorded under its b = 1 representation.)

The Hardy–Littlewood conjecture [5] makes the family quantitative. For the pair of quadratics f = a2+16, g = a2+18, the singular series 𝔖 = ∏ℓ (1 − ω(ℓ)/ℓ)(1 − 1/ℓ)−2 — with ω(ℓ) the number of roots of f g mod ℓ — evaluates to 𝔖 ≈ 3.8207 (product over ℓ < 105), giving the prediction 𝔖 · Σa ≤ 3161 (log f · log g)−1 ≈ 67.9 members below 107, against 68 observed. (Agreement this close is luck — the expected fluctuation is of order √68 ≈ 8 — but it confirms the family behaves exactly as conjectured.) Infinitude of the family is an instance of Schinzel's Hypothesis H [10] for two quadratics, and is open; indeed even a single quadratic, Landau's n2+1 problem, remains open. The fold is real and provably unique of its kind; what it transfers is the difficulty, undiminished.

5. No hidden correspondence: a wrong-partner control

If any algebraic correspondence beyond congruences linked the ℚ(i)-coordinates (a, b2) of p to the ℚ(√−2)-coordinates (x, y) of p+2, its statistical shadow should appear as dependence between the scale-free coordinates ã = a/√p, b̃² = b2/√p, x̃ = x/√p, ỹ = y/√p. (Hecke's theorem gives equidistribution of each side separately; the joint question is the open one.) Pearson correlations over the 1,227 pairs, with wrong-partner controls (each pair's left coordinates matched to the right coordinates of the k-th next pair):

The apparent 5-s.e. signal in corr(b̃², ỹ) under true pairing vanishes both under wrong-partner pairing and — decisively — when the 67 recorded members of the b = 2 family (for which x = a and y = 3 exactly) are removed. The residual dependence is consistent with zero.

Conclusion: at this scale, the entire measurable relationship between the two parameterizations is the single decoded family of Section 4. We find no evidence of any further correspondence — precisely the outcome the parity barrier and the orthogonality of digit/congruence structure to the Möbius function would lead one to expect [6, 12].

6. Discussion

The arc of this small experiment is the standard arc of twin-prime structure hunting in miniature, and that is why we record it. A suggestive framework (both members of the pair are norms) turns out to be congruence-forced; a genuinely surprising exact pattern (x = a, at 5 standard errors) decodes to a single diophantine coincidence (42+2 = 2·32) whose infinitude is equivalent to an instance of a conjecture as open as the one we started with; and the residual, once the coincidence is removed, is statistically indistinguishable from independence. Structure below the parity barrier is abundant, provable, and — as far as these measurements can see — exactly exhausted by what congruences force plus isolated diophantine accidents. This is consistent with the modern understanding of why the twin prime conjecture resists: distribution-level information provably certifies no positive lower bound on twin counts [6], and the known parity-breaking mechanisms attach to one structured sequence at a time [4], not to additive neighbors — a contrast made vivid by the function-field setting, where the Frobenius symmetry does see both members at once and the twin prime analogue is a theorem (Sawin–Shusterman [12]).

Everything here is reproducible from a single dependency-free Python script in the repository (experiments/census.py, ~2 minutes); the interactive visualization that prompted the investigation (index.html, "Primefold") is included alongside.

7. The complete fold table, and a second family

Section 4 searched for the fold x = a only inside the representation that p mod 8 dictates. The systematic question is: for which shifts s does the pair (a2+b4, a2+b4+s) fold with the parameter a passing through — i.e. when is b4 + s = m y2, so that the shifted member is a2 + m y2? For each pair (s, m) this is a quartic curve with finitely many integral points, so every fold is an isolated diophantine accident. Exhaustively, for even s ≤ 100, m ∈ {1, 2, 3, 7} (the class-number-one forms), b ≤ 300, there are 36 folds. At the twin shift s = 2 there are exactly two:

(m, b, y) = (3, 1, 1):  a2+1, a2+3 = a2+3·12   and   (2, 2, 3):  a2+16, a2+18 = a2+2·32.

The first — generated by the even smaller coincidence 1 + 2 = 3·12 — was invisible to the Section 4 search because its members with p ≡ 1 (mod 8) are recorded in the x2+2y2 representation, where nothing passes through. Its members begin (5, 7), (17, 19), (101, 103), (197, 199). Both families were counted to 109 against their Hardy–Littlewood predictions:

8. Gaps between FI primes: data for an uncovered question

Sections 2–7 concern an FI prime with an arbitrary prime neighbor. The sharper question in the spirit of bounded gaps: how close together do FI primes themselves come? Since every odd FI prime is ≡ 1 (mod 4), consecutive gaps are multiples of 4, so the "twin" distance for this sequence is 4. Below 109 (322,109 FI primes; counts here exclude p = 2 = 12+14):

 The decade growth ratios (2.68, 2.62) are consistent with the heuristic c·√x/(log x)² for pairs of an x^(3/4)-sparse sequence (which predicts 2.4–2.5), and the divisibility-rich popular gaps are the usual singular-series weighting. Data: experiments/beyond.py.

Why record this: for ordinary primes, "pairs within distance 246 at a positive rate" is the Zhang–Maynard–Polymath theorem [8, 9, 11]. For FI primes no analogous theorem appears to be known, and the sequence sits outside the direct reach of the Maynard–Tao machinery: it has relative density zero among the primes, while the general subset theorems require positive relative density [13]. Recent work on Gaussian primes in sparse sets [14] develops equidistribution of the required flavor, so the question — are there infinitely many pairs of FI primes within bounded distance? — may be difficult but is plausibly not parity-hard, since it asks for two primes each carrying FI structure rather than a structureless additive neighbor. The empirical rate is robustly positive at every scale we measured. We record the data and pose the question.
