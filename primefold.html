What the scope draws

Each integer n is written in an ordinary digit base D, then its digits are re-priced in a complex base: z(n) = Σ dₖβᵏ. At β = −1+i this enumerates the twindragon; at 2i, Knuth's quater-imaginary system; between lattice points it deforms continuously through fractal dust. The digit count is capped at ⌈log_D n⌉ — the scope shows a finite truncation of the infinite-digit fractal limit, which is the "base going to infinity" made concrete: add points and you add digits, refining the same self-similar shape.

Reading the alignment score

When R spikes during a scan, the twins really are lining up — but decode before celebrating. Twin pairs differ by 2, so z(p+2) − z(p) is governed by how adding 2 carries through the low digits of p, and carry patterns are periodic. Most alignment is the sieve's wheel structure (all twins > 3 sit at 6k±1) refracted through the digit map — visible structure, but structure we can already prove. The same goes for streaks in the Ulam spiral: they decode to quadratic progressions, i.e. Hardy–Littlewood territory.

What a real find would look like

Minsky–Papert proved the primes are never an exactly self-similar (finite-state) subset of any digit geometry, so no β can make them perfectly periodic — the residue beyond carry-and-wheel structure is where the twin prime conjecture lives. The hunt worth having: an alignment that persists as n grows and does not decode to congruences. Ulam found his spiral doodling in a meeting; Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan found the consecutive-prime bias in 2016 by computing a statistic everyone assumed was flat. Instruments first, theorems later.
